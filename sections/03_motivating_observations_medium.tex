\section{Motivating Observations}
\label{sec:observation}

Before developing our theoretical framework, we present key empirical observations that motivate the Information Budget framework. These observations reveal puzzles that homophily alone cannot explain.

\subsection{The U-Shape Pattern}

We conduct controlled synthetic experiments on the Contextual Stochastic Block Model (CSBM) with $n=1000$ nodes, varying homophily $h \in \{0.1, 0.2, ..., 0.9\}$ while keeping features constant (MLP accuracy $\approx 99\%$).

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{u_shape_pub}
\caption{GNN accuracy forms a U-shape as homophily varies, while MLP remains constant. The valley at $h=0.5$ represents maximum aggregation damage.}
\label{fig:ushape}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{U-Shape Severity by Model Architecture}
\label{tab:ushape}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Valley} & \textbf{Peak} & \textbf{Amplitude} \\
\midrule
GCN & Replace & $-18.75\%$ & $+0.60\%$ & \textbf{19.35\%} \\
GAT & Replace+Attn & $-5.30\%$ & $+0.55\%$ & 5.85\% \\
GraphSAGE & Concat & $-0.10\%$ & $+0.65\%$ & \textbf{0.75\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key Finding 1} (Figure~\ref{fig:ushape}, Table~\ref{tab:ushape}): GNN performance follows a U-shape with minimum at $h=0.5$. The amplitude varies dramatically by architecture:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GCN}: 19.35\% amplitude---aggregation severely damages performance at mid-homophily
    \item \textbf{GAT}: 5.85\% amplitude---attention reduces but does not eliminate damage
    \item \textbf{GraphSAGE}: 0.75\% amplitude---concatenation nearly eliminates the U-shape
\end{itemize}

This suggests the critical factor is \textit{whether the model preserves original features}. GCN replaces node features with aggregated neighbors, while GraphSAGE concatenates, preserving access to original information.

\subsection{Same Homophily, Opposite Outcomes}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Same Homophily, Different GNN Outcomes}
\label{tab:same_h}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{$h$} & \textbf{$k$} & \textbf{MLP} & \textbf{Budget} & \textbf{GCN Adv.} \\
\midrule
Cora & 0.81 & 3.9 & 75.1\% & 24.9\% & \textbf{+12.7\%} \\
Coauthor-CS & 0.81 & 8.9 & 94.4\% & 5.6\% & $-0.7\%$ \\
\midrule
CiteSeer & 0.74 & 2.7 & 73.8\% & 26.2\% & +3.2\% \\
PubMed & 0.80 & 4.5 & 87.6\% & 12.4\% & +0.2\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key Finding 2} (Table~\ref{tab:same_h}): Datasets with identical or similar homophily show vastly different GNN outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cora ($h=0.81$): GCN gains +12.7\%
    \item Coauthor-CS ($h=0.81$): GCN \textit{loses} 0.7\%
\end{itemize}

The difference is \textit{feature sufficiency}: Cora's MLP achieves 75.1\%, leaving 24.9\% ``budget'' for improvement. Coauthor-CS's MLP achieves 94.4\%, leaving only 5.6\% budget. When features nearly solve the task, there is little room for structure to help.

\textbf{Descriptive Observation}: The Cora/Coauthor-CS comparison motivates feature headroom as a diagnostic, but the audited nine-dataset external set does not show a reliable linear correlation between Budget and best-GNN advantage ($r=-0.255$, bootstrap CI crosses zero). We therefore treat this pair as motivation rather than population-level statistical evidence.

\subsection{The Heterophily Paradox}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Low-Homophily Datasets Show Inconsistent GNN Behavior}
\label{tab:heterophily_paradox}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{$h$} & \textbf{MLP} & \textbf{GCN} & \textbf{H2GCN} & \textbf{Best} \\
\midrule
Texas & 0.087 & 80.5\% & 56.3\% & 86.8\% & H2GCN \\
Actor & 0.22 & 36.4\% & 29.5\% & 35.9\% & MLP \\
Chameleon & 0.24 & 49.0\% & 64.6\% & 63.6\% & GCN \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key Finding 3} (Table~\ref{tab:heterophily_paradox}): Heterophilic datasets behave inconsistently:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Texas}: H2GCN is 6.3 percentage points above MLP (paired bootstrap 95\% CI: 1.3--11.1 points), while GCN underperforms
    \item \textbf{Actor}: MLP wins, all GNNs fail
    \item \textbf{Chameleon}: GCN wins (+15.6\% over MLP), H2GCN underperforms GCN
\end{itemize}

This paradox cannot be explained by homophily alone. Section~\ref{sec:heterophily} tests two-hop recovery as one descriptive mechanism, while showing that it does not resolve model selection by itself.

\subsection{Summary: Three Puzzles Motivating Our Framework}

These observations reveal three puzzles that homophily alone cannot explain:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{U-Shape Puzzle}: Why does GNN performance form a U-shape with homophily? Why is the amplitude architecture-dependent?

    \item \textbf{Same-$h$ Puzzle}: Why do datasets with identical homophily show opposite GNN outcomes?

    \item \textbf{Heterophily Puzzle}: Why do some low-$h$ datasets benefit from GNNs while others don't? Why does H2GCN help on Texas but not Actor?
\end{enumerate}

The Information Budget framework (Sections~\ref{sec:snr}--\ref{sec:heterophily}) provides unified answers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{U-Shape}: The exact fixed-degree ratio is zero at $h=0.5$ ($\rho=0$), predicting maximal loss of neighbor-label signal
    \item \textbf{Same-$h$}: Feature sufficiency determines the ceiling; structure can only utilize what features leave unexplained
    \item \textbf{Heterophily}: two-hop recovery describes longer-range label structure but does not determine whether MLP or a particular graph model wins
\end{itemize}
