\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Unified Understanding of GNN Behavior}

The scoped calculation and empirical diagnostics suggest possible explanations for several GNN phenomena, while stopping short of architecture-level prediction:

\textbf{(1) The U-Shape Pattern.} The fixed-degree calculation predicts minimal neighbor-label discriminability when $\rho = 2h-1 \approx 0$. This offers a mechanism for, but does not by itself prove, the empirical GNN accuracy dip at mid-homophily.

\textbf{(2) Same-$h$ differences.} Datasets with similar homophily can yield different GNN outcomes because a global edge-label statistic omits class-conditional neighborhood patterns, feature geometry, degree variation, and optimization effects. Two-hop homophily and MLP accuracy are descriptive diagnostics, not factors proven to be equally important.

\textbf{(3) Architecture dependence.} An explicit ego channel changes the representation available to a downstream classifier, but this chain-rule observation does not imply that GraphSAGE, GCN, or attention will be uniformly preferable after finite-sample training. Recent CSBM work also finds that attention benefits depend on feature and structural noise~\cite{pmlr-v267-ma25w}.

\subsection{Connection to Existing Theory}

Our moment calculation is adjacent to, but distinct from, several theoretical traditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tree broadcasting} \cite{kesten1966additional,mossel2015reconstruction}: A branching-factor--times--squared-correlation quantity appears in reconstruction on trees. Its resemblance to the weak-feature limit of our ratio is contextual only; fixed neighbor count, regular-tree forward degree, and Poisson offspring are different objects.

    \item \textbf{Finite-depth CSBM embeddings}: Dalle and Thiran~\cite{pmlr-v269-dalle25a} obtain Gaussian-mixture embeddings for a linearized GCN and report depth behavior more complex than a single-Gaussian approximation. Our one-hop proposition neither characterizes oversmoothing nor supplies a depth threshold.

    \item \textbf{CSBM recovery theory}: Sharp detection and weak-recovery thresholds have been established for the CSBM~\cite{lu2023contextual}. Our conditional moment ratio has a different target and is not presented as a recovery threshold.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

Our framework has important scope conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary fixed-degree focus}: The proposition assumes binary labels, a fixed neighbor count, conditionally independent neighbor labels, Gaussian feature noise, and a shared positive-definite covariance. We state no scalar multi-class extension.

    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Assumptions}: We assume uniform within-class and between-class edge probabilities. Real graphs may have heterogeneous community structure.

    \item \textbf{No universal transition interval}: The exact threshold is internal to the stated moment model and depends on $(d,s)$. The experiments do not establish fixed homophily intervals that transfer across datasets or architectures.

    \item \textbf{Finite graphs}: Random degree, isolated nodes, cycles, neighborhood reuse, and estimation error are not covered by the fixed-degree proposition. The available evidence does not identify a universal sample-size cutoff.

    \item \textbf{Feature Distribution}: Gaussian feature assumption enables closed-form analysis but may not hold for categorical or sparse features.

    \item \textbf{Static Graphs}: We analyze static topology; dynamic or temporal graphs require additional considerations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Finite-Sample Considerations}

Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed_degree} is a finite fixed-degree moment calculation. Extending it to sparse finite graphs requires handling random degree, isolated nodes, cycles, and dependence introduced by repeated aggregation.

\textbf{Sample-size considerations.} The available experiments are not sufficient to infer a universal numerical sample-size cutoff:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Large graphs}: one successful three-seed ogbn-arxiv run is a descriptive scalability check, not evidence of transfer to other OGB datasets.
    \item \textbf{Small graphs}: the WebKB results show appreciable variation, but the current artifact does not isolate graph size from split, label, feature, and structural effects.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Finite-$n$ Error Bounds.} We do not claim a finite-$n$ rate for transferring the fixed-degree proposition to CSBM. Such a result would require a separate coupling argument and an explicit treatment of the random-degree mixture.

\textbf{Practical use.} For any dataset, the current quantities should be treated as exploratory diagnostics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item report the MLP--GNN accuracy difference with uncertainty over seeds or splits;
    \item report one-hop and two-hop label statistics separately rather than using a fixed selection threshold; and
    \item validate any decision rule prospectively on datasets not used to construct it.
\end{enumerate}

For large-scale graphs, the current audited artifact contains a successful 3-seed run on ogbn-arxiv (169K nodes). Broader million-node validation remains future work; the available ogbn-products run terminated with an out-of-memory error and is not used as evidence here.

The prospective diagnostic benchmark has not yet been executed on newly generated model records. Its frozen scorer and leakage checks improve auditability, but they are infrastructure rather than empirical evidence. Likewise, the verified double-edge-swap implementation has not regenerated the legacy edge-randomization outcomes.

\subsection{Broader Impact}

\textbf{Positive Applications}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Supporting targeted investigation of aggregation failures
    \item Making architecture comparisons easier to audit
    \item Providing interpretable diagnostics for GNN failures
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Potential Misuse}: The framework could be used to identify datasets where GNNs are vulnerable to adversarial structure manipulation. We note that such attacks require graph edit access, which is typically restricted.

\subsection{Baseline Method Selection}

Our experimental evaluation focuses on representative architectures that span the design space:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Replace aggregation}: GCN, GAT (with attention)
    \item \textbf{Concatenation}: GraphSAGE
    \item \textbf{Heterophily-aware}: H2GCN, LINKX
    \item \textbf{Adaptive propagation}: GPR-GNN
\end{itemize}

We acknowledge that recent methods (FAGCN~\cite{fagcn2021}, GloGNN~\cite{li2022glognn}, ACM-GCN~\cite{acmgcn2024}) offer additional innovations. The selected baselines span several aggregation designs, but they do not establish architecture-independent predictions. They provide descriptive comparisons of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The U-shape pattern (GCN vs GraphSAGE)
    \item accuracy-difference patterns (replace vs concatenate)
    \item Heterophily handling (H2GCN, LINKX)
\end{enumerate}

Future work should test any diagnostic rule on emerging architectures and held-out datasets before claiming model-selection value.

\subsection{Future Directions}

Several extensions warrant investigation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Beyond CSBM}: Analyzing random geometric graphs, preferential attachment, and real-world degree distributions.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive Architectures}: Deriving estimators and uncertainty for the scoped quantities before testing whether they can safely adjust aggregation depth or interact with attention.

    \item \textbf{Semi-Supervised Learning}: Extending the framework to account for label scarcity and propagation effects.

    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous Graphs}: Adapting the SNR analysis to multi-relational graphs with different edge types.
\end{enumerate}
