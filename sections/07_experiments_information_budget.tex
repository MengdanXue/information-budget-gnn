\section{Experimental Validation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We validate the Information Budget framework through controlled synthetic experiments, real-world dataset analysis, and ablation studies.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Synthetic Data (CSBM)}: Contextual Stochastic Block Model with $n=1000$ nodes, $d=20$ features, and controlled homophily $h \in \{0.1, ..., 0.9\}$. Feature separability is set to achieve MLP accuracy $\approx 99\%$, creating maximum budget scenario.

\textbf{Real Datasets}: We use 19 benchmark datasets spanning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Citation}: Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed, DBLP, ogbn-arxiv
    \item \textbf{Co-authorship}: Coauthor-CS, Coauthor-Physics
    \item \textbf{Wikipedia}: Chameleon, Squirrel, Actor
    \item \textbf{WebKB}: Texas, Wisconsin, Cornell
    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous}: Roman-empire, Amazon-ratings
    \item \textbf{Large-scale}: ogbn-products (2.4M nodes)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Models}: MLP, GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE, H2GCN, LINKX, APPNP, GPR-GNN.

\textbf{Protocol}: 10 random seeds, stratified train/val/test splits (60/20/20). Statistical significance via Wilcoxon signed-rank test ($\alpha = 0.05$).

\subsubsection{Understanding Negative Gains and Loose Ceilings}
\label{sec:negative_gain}

Two phenomena require careful interpretation: (1) negative GNN gains (GNN worse than MLP), and (2) large slackness (actual gain far below ceiling).

\textbf{Negative Gains Are Not Violations}: When $\Delta = \text{Acc}_{\text{GNN}} - \text{Acc}_{\text{MLP}} < 0$, the ceiling bound $\Delta \leq \mathcal{B}$ is trivially satisfied. However, negative gains provide important diagnostic information:

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Negative Gain Analysis: GNN Performs Worse Than MLP}
\label{tab:negative_gains}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{$h$} & \textbf{Budget} & \textbf{GCN Adv.} & \textbf{Diagnosis} \\
\midrule
Actor & 0.22 & 63.6\% & $-6.9\%$ & Type B heterophily \\
Coauthor-CS & 0.81 & 5.6\% & $-0.7\%$ & Budget-limited \\
Coauthor-Physics & 0.93 & 4.2\% & $+0.6\%$ & Near-zero ceiling \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Interpretation}: Negative gains indicate that aggregation \textit{damages} information (ADR $> 0$). This occurs when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structure is misleading}: Type B heterophily (neighbors provide no useful signal)
    \item \textbf{Aggregation destroys features}: Mid-homophily maximizes information loss
    \item \textbf{Optimization fails}: Model cannot learn to utilize structure
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Loose Ceilings Are Expected}: The ceiling $\mathcal{B}$ represents the \textit{maximum possible} improvement, not guaranteed improvement. Large slackness (e.g., Actor: $\mathcal{B} = 63.6\%$, $\Delta = -6.9\%$, slackness = 70.5\%) indicates:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Slackness} = \mathcal{B} - \Delta = \underbrace{(1 - \eta) \cdot \mathcal{B}}_{\text{Unutilized budget}} + \underbrace{|\min(\Delta, 0)|}_{\text{Damage if } \Delta < 0}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Two-Stage Interpretation}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Ceiling)}: $\mathcal{B}$ answers ``how much room exists for structure to help?''
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Efficiency)}: $\eta(h)$ answers ``how much of this room can be utilized?''
\end{enumerate}

The ceiling is not ``too loose''---it correctly identifies the \textit{upper bound}. The gap between ceiling and actual gain is explained by structural utilization efficiency $\eta$, which depends on homophily and architecture choice.

\begin{remark}[On Threshold Calibration: $h = 0.3$ and $h = 0.7$]
\label{rem:threshold_calibration}
The regime boundaries $h = 0.3$ and $h = 0.7$ are \textbf{empirically calibrated}, not theoretically derived constants. Their origin:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical motivation}: From Observation~\ref{obs:ail_scaling}, AIL $\propto 4h(1-h)$, which peaks at $h=0.5$. The thresholds roughly correspond to where AIL drops to $\sim 84\%$ of maximum (at $h=0.3$: $4 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.84$).
    \item \textbf{Empirical validation}: Grid search over $\{0.2, 0.25, ..., 0.45\} \times \{0.55, 0.6, ..., 0.8\}$ identified $(0.3, 0.7)$ as maximizing prediction accuracy on CSBM (Table~\ref{tab:threshold_sensitivity}).
    \item \textbf{Robustness}: $\pm 0.05$ perturbation degrades accuracy by only 2.8--5.6\%, indicating the choice is not brittle.
\end{enumerate}
We emphasize these are \textbf{practical guidelines}, not universal constants. Different domains may require recalibration.
\end{remark}

The phase diagram (Table~\ref{tab:phase_diagram_summary}) summarizes the distinct performance regimes in the $(h, \text{Budget})$ parameter space.

\textbf{Empirical Observation}: The phase diagram reveals three distinct regions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GNN-Beneficial Region} (upper-right, green): High budget AND (high $h$ OR low $h$ with Type A heterophily). GNNs consistently outperform MLP.
    \item \textbf{GNN-Harmful Region} (center, red): Mid-homophily ($h \approx 0.5$) regardless of budget. Maximum ADR causes aggregation damage.
    \item \textbf{Feature-Sufficient Region} (bottom, gray): Low budget (high MLP accuracy). GNN improvement ceiling is too low to matter.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Theory-Experiment Alignment}: The theoretical predictions from our framework---the Conditional Enhancement Bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:conditional_bound}), ADR peak at $h=0.5$ (Observation~\ref{obs:adr_peak}), and Dual Heterophily criterion (Proposition~\ref{prop:two_type})---jointly define boundary curves that align with the empirical regime transitions observed in the phase diagram. This alignment validates our theoretical framework.

\begin{remark}[Practical Interpretation of Phase Diagram]
The phase diagram provides practitioners with an intuitive decision surface: given a new dataset, compute MLP accuracy (to determine Budget) and homophily $h$ (to locate horizontal position), then read off the expected GNN behavior from the corresponding region. This transforms our theoretical framework into a practical diagnostic tool.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{When Our Framework Fails: Explicit Conditions}

Based on failure analysis, we identify conditions under which predictions become unreliable:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Transition zones}: $0.25 < h < 0.35$ or $0.65 < h < 0.75$ (near regime boundaries)
    \item \textbf{Extreme class imbalance}: Positive ratio $< 10\%$ or $> 90\%$
    \item \textbf{Very small graphs}: $|V| < 500$ nodes (insufficient for statistical averaging)
    \item \textbf{Highly irregular degree distributions}: Gini coefficient $> 0.6$
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Falsifiability Statement]
Our framework makes the following \textbf{falsifiable predictions}: (1) GNN advantage should be bounded by $(1 - \text{Acc}_{\text{MLP}})$ on any dataset satisfying the scope conditions; (2) ADR should peak near $h = 0.5$ for mean-aggregation GNNs; (3) Type A heterophily ($R > 1.5$) should show H2GCN gains $> 15\%$ over GCN. Systematic violations of these predictions would refute our theory.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Methodology}: We apply three feature transformations to test whether the 0\% opposite neighbor rate is due to non-negative features (BOW/TF-IDF):
\begin{enumerate}

\label{fig:noon_summary}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Implication}: The NOON finding fundamentally changes how we understand heterophily. Real heterophilic graphs do not have ``opposite'' neighbors that could provide contrastive signal---they have \textbf{orthogonal} neighbors that contribute noise. This explains why the U-shape theoretically predicted for opposite neighbors (Theorem~\ref{thm:ushape_condition}, Case 1) is not observed in practice: the prerequisite condition (FCS $< -\epsilon$) does not hold in real graphs.

\textbf{Key Insight}: This is a \textit{geometric artifact of rotation}, not a NOON violation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Original Actor features show 0\% negative (NOON holds for raw data)
    \item Random rotation is an artificial transformation not present in practice
    \item Even after rotation, 98.5\% of Actor pairs remain non-opposite
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[NOON Scope and Limitations]
\label{rem:noon_scope}
Based on our comprehensive experiments (9 datasets, 20 rotations, 13 counter-examples searched), we establish the following scope for NOON:

\textbf{What NOON Proves}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Real-world graph features (BOW/TF-IDF, one-hot) lack strongly opposite neighbors (cos$<$-0.5) in their \textit{original} form
    \item This holds across citation, webpage, social, and Wikipedia graphs
    \item The phenomenon is robust to centering and whitening transformations
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What NOON Does Not Prove}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item NOON does not claim arbitrary feature transformations preserve non-opposition
    \item Extreme sparsity (like Actor: 5.4 non-zeros/row) is a boundary condition where rotation can create artifacts
    \item NOON applies to graph-structured data; random feature matrices may behave differently
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Practical Implication}: Since GNNs operate on original features (not rotated), NOON directly applies to real GNN applications. The Actor boundary case reveals the geometric mechanism but does not invalidate the phenomenon for practical use.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Practical Application Guide: From Theory to Practice}
\label{sec:practical_guide}

Based on our theoretical framework and experimental validation, we provide a complete practical workflow for applying SNR theory to new datasets.

\subsubsection{Step-by-Step Algorithm for Model Selection}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{SNR-Based GNN Model Selection}
\label{alg:snr_model_selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Graph $G = (V, E)$, node features $X$, labels $Y$ (training set)
\Ensure Recommended model and expected performance
\State \textbf{Step 1: Compute Basic Statistics}
\State $h \gets |\{(u,v) \in E : Y_u = Y_v\}| / |E|$ \Comment{Homophily}
\State $k \gets 2|E| / |V|$ \Comment{Average degree}
\State $\rho \gets 2h - 1$ \Comment{Edge correlation}
\State $\kappa \gets \rho^2 \cdot k$ \Comment{SNR ratio}
\State
\State \textbf{Step 2: Train MLP Baseline}
\State $\text{Acc}_{\text{MLP}} \gets$ \Call{TrainMLP}{$X$, $Y$}
\State $\mathcal{B} \gets 1 - \text{Acc}_{\text{MLP}}$ \Comment{Information Budget}
\State
\State \textbf{Step 3: Decision Logic}
\If{$\mathcal{B} < 0.05$}
    \State \Return ``MLP sufficient (Budget too small)''
\ElsIf{$\kappa > 1$ and $h > 0.7$}
    \State \Return ``GCN or GraphSAGE (High-$h$, beneficial aggregation)''
\ElsIf{$\kappa > 1$ and $h < 0.3$}
    \State Compute $R_{2\text{-hop}} \gets h_2 / h_1$
    \If{$R_{2\text{-hop}} > 1.5$}
        \State \Return ``H2GCN or GPR-GNN (Type A heterophily)''
    \Else
        \State \Return ``MLP or LINKX (Type B heterophily)''
    \EndIf
\ElsIf{$0.3 < h < 0.7$}
    \State \Return ``GraphSAGE (Mid-$h$, use concatenation to avoid damage)''
\Else
    \State \Return ``MLP baseline (uncertain regime)''
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Case Study: Applying the Framework}

\textbf{Scenario}: A practitioner has a new social network graph for fraud detection.

\textbf{Step 1}: Compute statistics: $h = 0.35$, $k = 12$, giving $\kappa = (0.7)^2 \times 12 = 0.09 < 1$.

\textbf{Step 2}: Train MLP: $\text{Acc}_{\text{MLP}} = 82\%$, so $\mathcal{B} = 18\%$.

\textbf{Step 3}: Decision: Mid-$h$ regime with $\kappa < 1$. Recommend GraphSAGE to avoid aggregation damage. Expected improvement: $18\% \times 0.1 = 1.8\%$, giving estimated accuracy $\approx 83.8\%$.

\textbf{Step 4}: If higher performance needed, compute $R_{2\text{-hop}}$. If $> 1.5$, try H2GCN for potential $18\% \times 0.4 = 7.2\%$ improvement.

\subsubsection{Handling Class Imbalance and Multi-Class Settings}

For settings beyond balanced binary classification:

\textbf{Class Imbalance}: When positive ratio $< 20\%$, use stratified metrics (F1, AUC-ROC) instead of accuracy. The budget principle still applies:
\begin{equation}
\text{GNN F1 Improvement} \leq 1 - \text{F1}_{\text{MLP}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Multi-Class ($C > 2$)}: The effective edge correlation becomes:
\begin{equation}
\rho_C = \frac{C \cdot h - 1}{C - 1}
\end{equation}
For $C = 7$ (Cora) and $h = 0.81$: $\rho_7 = 0.78$, $\kappa_7 = 2.37 > 1$.
